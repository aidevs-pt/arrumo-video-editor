# Data stories and recaps

- Every figure comes from the brief, the script or the creator's data, exactly as given and rounded only as far as the source allows. Check each number on the final frames: a wrong digit is worse than any layout flaw.
- Choose the chart for the question: change over time as a line, a ranking as bars, a share of a whole as a ring or a row of units, a comparison as two bars on one scale. One large number is a chart too.
- Let the viewer read the frame before it moves: hold the axes and units for about a second, then build one series at a time. Viewers can follow only one moving thing.
- Animate the values, not only the opacity: a figure counts up and settles on its value, bars grow from their baseline one after another, a line draws from left to right and its area fills in after it. Quantities never overshoot and axes stay still. Count at a steady speed only for a running clock or a year.
- Give the key moment extra time: slow down at the crossing, the record or the turning point, mark it with one callout, then let the callout go. Label values where they sit and state units once.
- Infographic scenes: design the finished frame first, then reveal it in order of importance: the headline alone, then for each item its icon, its number and its label, then the line leading to the next item. One central insight with three to five supporting figures, and only one number counting at a time.
- Hold the finished chart or composition for two to three seconds. It is the frame people pause on and share.
- Rankings that change over time: move smoothly between data points so bars glide past each other, keep about a dozen at most and gather the rest as "other", and keep each item's colour throughout.
- Recaps about one person or team: write in the second person ("You shipped 48 releases this year"), order the figures from modest to most impressive, give the biggest one the most space and time, say what the figures mean about them, and end on a still card worth sharing. Never open with the biggest number.
- Maps: establish the place with a slow move, mark places in the order the story visits them, draw routes in reading order, and light the region in question while dimming the rest. Keep labels upright and short. Draw only map shapes you actually have.
- A colour means one thing for the whole video; never give it a new meaning halfway.

Checks: every number matches its source; one thing moves at a time; the key moment is marked; the final state holds still; colours keep their meaning.
