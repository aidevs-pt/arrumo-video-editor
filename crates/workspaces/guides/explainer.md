# Explainers and tutorials

- Find the one idea a viewer could pass on to a friend in a single sentence. When you cannot write that sentence, narrow the topic; drop what does not serve the idea rather than squeezing it in.
- Words come first: the narration carries the video and each picture shows the line being said. At a relaxed pace a minute holds about 140 spoken words; slow down for figures and technical terms.
- Shape: the problem in the viewer's own words, what it costs them, the answer in one line, how it works in one to three concrete steps, then the result and one next step. Spend most of the time on the steps, roughly a fifth each on the problem and the result.
- Pace follows the tension: move briskly through the problem, give the answer a moment of stillness, slow down for each step and let the ending breathe.
- Choose one comparison from the viewer's everyday life, such as a queue, a recipe or a thermostat, and keep it for the whole video in both pictures and words. If the comparison needs explaining itself, choose another.
- Show cause and effect rather than labels: a step fills, moves or counts because of the step before. A counter or gauge that stays on screen across scenes turns separate steps into a chain.
- Build diagrams progressively: the outline faintly first, then the parts in logical order, then the connections drawing between them, then the labels. Light the active part and dim the rest, and hold each step about 0.5–1.5 s or until the narration moves on. Movement along a connector shows which way things flow.
- Tutorials: one step per scene, numbered when the viewer needs a map. Show the result first when it motivates the steps. Zoom towards the part of the screen in use, up to about twice its size, and hold there; move the view or the pointer, never both; show one callout at a time.
- Presentations turned into video: each scene makes one claim and its heading states the claim ("Late invoices cost us a month a year"), not a topic ("Invoices"). Open on a question, a surprising figure or what is at stake, never an agenda. When one point needs long narration, split it into two scenes instead of shrinking the text.
- Hand-drawn, isometric or blueprint looks are styles, not structures. Choose one only when it suits the audience, and keep it for the whole video.
- Finish by restating the chain in a single sentence, then give one clear next step.

Checks: the one idea is said and shown; every scene shows the line being spoken; steps arrive in order and are held long enough to read; the comparison never changes; the final scene names the next step.
